# Banana Ripening Behavior

Bananas are a climacteric fruit: after harvest they continue to ripen through a
sharp, self-sustaining rise in respiration and ethylene production. Once this
climacteric rise begins, ripening is irreversible - it cannot be paused or
reversed by returning the fruit to cooler storage, only slowed.

Commercial ripening is typically induced deliberately: bananas are exposed to
100-150 ppm ethylene gas for 24-48 hours at 15-20°C (59-68°F) and 90-95%
relative humidity to produce uniform ripening across a batch. Carbon dioxide is
kept below 1% during this process because CO2 delays ethylene's ripening effect.

Practical implication for shelf-life reasoning: a banana batch already showing
visible yellowing/ripening in CV images has already entered the climacteric
stage and will continue ripening toward over-ripeness/spoilage regardless of
subsequent cooling - cooling only slows the rate, it does not reset the clock.

A cut banana crown (stem end) is the primary site of ethylene emission from the
bunch; a small number of fingers ripening early in a batch can accelerate
ripening in adjacent fingers due to this ethylene exposure.
